Subject: Adapting the Social Work Research Database method to suicide research
Dear Brian,

I read your Research on Social Work Practice paper on 35 years of social work knowledge production, and it prompted me to build the equivalent infrastructure for suicide research. I am writing because the work follows your method closely enough that it would be strange not to tell you, and because I think you would improve it.

The adaptation turned out to be less mechanical than I expected. Social work is defined by its journals; suicide research is not. There are roughly five dedicated suicidology journals in the world, so a venue-based corpus captures a small and biased slice of the field. I ended up with a hybrid: complete venue capture of the specialty journals plus a title-focused topical sweep across every other venue, with each record tagged by tier. That produced the finding I did not expect, which is that 5.1 percent of suicide research appears in dedicated suicidology journals, and that share has not moved since 1989.

The corpus is 96,641 records from 1989 to 2025 across 5,863 journals, built entirely on PubMed, Europe PMC, OpenAlex, and NIH iCite, with no Web of Science or Scopus in any reported result. I diverged from you on classification: rather than a 20B local model, I used NLM PublicationType tags as distant supervision for a linear classifier, which let me validate against human indexing on tens of thousands of held-out records rather than a hundred. Hold-out kappa is 0.79 for empirical status and 0.95 for methodology. I have also shipped an Ollama backend that reproduces your design directly, and I would like to run both and report the comparison.

I would be glad to have you as a coauthor. The contribution I have in mind is the scientometric framing and the comparison between the two fields, which is the part where your judgment is worth more than mine. If you would rather stay at arm's length and simply comment, that is also welcome, and I will acknowledge you either way.

The draft, the code, and the data are ready to share whenever you would like to see them.

Best,
Yunyu

Non-English scholarship and journals from low- and middle-income countries are under-represented in every source I used, which is precisely where the burden falls. I would be honored to have SSG's read on that, as a coauthor.

Everything is open: code, data, and a draft that rebuilds its own numbers from the corpus.

Within that portion, treatment-level research outweighs universal prevention by roughly sixteen to one. Most people who die by suicide are not in current psychiatric treatment, so the literature is concentrated where a minority of the deaths are. Separately, pesticide ingestion is among the leading global methods and accounts for 1.3 percent of the corpus, against 2.7 percent for firearms, a ratio that tracks research funding rather than mortality.

The instrument behind those numbers is the Suicide Research Evidence Database, which adapts the method Perron and colleagues used for social work earlier this year. It integrates PubMed, Europe PMC, OpenAlex, and NIH iCite into a deduplicated corpus covering 5,863 journals, classifies each abstract by empirical status and methodology against human NLM indexing, and extracts fields no bibliographic database carries: prevention level, outcome construct, population, study design, lethal means, and social determinants of health. Subject: Invitation: classification validation for a 96,000-record suicide research database
Dear [Name],

I am writing to ask you to join a paper as a coauthor, with a specific and bounded job.

The Suicide Research Evidence Database integrates PubMed, Europe PMC, OpenAlex, and NIH iCite into 96,641 deduplicated suicide research records spanning 1989 to 2025. Each abstract is classified in three stages, scientific communication, then empirical status, then methodology, following Perron and colleagues. Where they used a 20 billion parameter local model, I used NLM PublicationType tags as distant supervision for a calibrated linear classifier, which has the advantage that validation runs against human indexers on tens of thousands of held-out records rather than a hand-coded hundred.

Hold-out kappa is 0.35 for scientific communication, 0.79 for empirical status, and 0.95 for methodology. Leave-one-decade-out kappa for empirical status ranges 0.71 to 0.81. The 0.35 is the honest weak point and I report it as such: requiring a substantive abstract removes almost all editorials and letters before classification runs, so what survives is by construction the non-scientific writing that reads like research.

What I would like from you is the confirmatory human coding. There is a 300 record sample stratified by decade and predicted methodology, in a template with blank columns for an independent coder. Double-coding it is the one part of this the pipeline cannot do for itself, and it is what the manuscript will report as the confirmatory kappa. A second job, if you want it, is running the Ollama backend at corpus scale so we can report the language model and distant supervision label sets head to head on the same records.

Everything is versioned and reproducible: code, config, data, and a manuscript that substitutes its own numbers from the analysis output at build time, so the prose cannot drift from the data.
